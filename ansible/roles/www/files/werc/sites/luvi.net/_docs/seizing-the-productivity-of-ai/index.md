# NNJ DSA and AI: Seizing the Productivity of AI

## Introduction

This document is for members of the NNJ DSA Tech Committee, any chapter organizer, and socialists in office who want a minimum program for AI under democratic public ownership. It presumes familiarity with how models are trained and deployed, but not with socialist political economy; technical and theoretical terms are defined on first use. It stress-tests Richard Eskow's argument that large language models are a collective creation and that socialism is the only fair way to govern them <sup><a href="#fn1" id="ref1">1</a></sup>. That is a political question, not a side issue for trade unions or tech policy alone <sup><a href="#fn2" id="ref2">2</a></sup> <sup><a href="#fn3" id="ref3">3</a></sup> <sup><a href="#fn4" id="ref4">4</a></sup>.

A handful of corporations now control the compute, the training data, and the accumulated labor of millions of workers and creators, then capture the resulting gains as private profit.<sup><a href="#fn5" id="ref5">5</a></sup> Public ownership alone does not resolve that accumulation of power. A state can expropriate AI infrastructure (transfer it into public ownership without compensation) and still extract creative labor without consent. It can still ignore ecological limits and still insulate decisions from democratic control. When it does, it has changed the owner, not the relationship.

The argument that follows tests Eskow's claim against increasingly demanding standards. It begins with non-negotiable protections for artists and other creative workers, whose labor is the clearest case of expropriation without consent. From there it develops a tiered framework, the Collective Chimera, for distinguishing general social knowledge from specific creative and personal contributions. That framework is then measured against ecosocialist demands, asking whether seizing AI's productivity is compatible with binding material limits. These conclusions are consolidated into a single minimum program: what to expropriate, resist, build, guarantee, and democratize. The document closes by naming the strategic questions this program cannot yet answer, leaving them to organizers as open work.

## Minimum Non-Negotiables for Artist-Aligned Socialists

A socialist AI program that does not liquidate the artist must include the following protections.

### Sectoral Bargaining Rights

Individual "opt-out" mechanisms are impractical because they shift responsibility onto creators to monitor and challenge large corporations.<sup><a href="#fn6" id="ref6">6</a></sup> The appropriate solution is sectoral collective bargaining: unions and professional organizations negotiate the terms under which members' work enters training data. Writers' organizations, actors' unions with rank-and-file-elected leadership, musicians' federations, and journalists' associations need both legal and practical authority to conduct those negotiations. A measured approach requires: prohibiting training on copyrighted material without a sectoral agreement, making exclusion the default, and requiring ratification of agreements through membership votes rather than closed-door deals.

### Public Attribution Infrastructure

A socialist AI program must build public content-provenance infrastructure: watermarking and cryptographic signing so creators can verify whether their work was used, plus training data registries. These systems would let creators trace when and how corporations have used their work. The work is technically hard. Content-provenance standards such as C2PA rely partly on watermarks and fingerprints, which remain vulnerable to adversarial removal and to forgery attacks that make manipulated content appear authentic<sup><a href="#fn7" id="ref7">7</a></sup><sup><a href="#fn8" id="ref8">8</a></sup>. Without robust versions of this infrastructure, organizers cannot validate claims of democratic control.

### General Social Knowledge and Creative Labor

Marx's concept of the "general intellect" in the *Grundrisse* names the collective accumulation of scientific, technical, and social knowledge embodied in machinery and social organization<sup><a href="#fn9" id="ref9">9</a></sup><sup><a href="#fn10" id="ref10">10</a></sup>. In plain terms, it is knowledge built up across generations that no single person produced. Engels describes how social experience becomes inherited collective knowledge<sup><a href="#fn10">10</a></sup>. Marx writes that "general social knowledge has become a direct force of production."<sup><a href="#fn9">9</a></sup> That knowledge is collective and cannot be attributed to individual creators. Specific creative outputs (a voice performance, a script, an investigative article) are products of identifiable labor and should not be classified as general social knowledge.

Corporations often expropriate these works without consent.<sup><a href="#fn6">6</a></sup> A socialist program must recognize both categories and apply distinct policies: treat the general intellect as a commons; demand explicit consent and compensation for individual creative works.

### Automation Pensions

Eskow endorses Nathan J. Robinson's "automation pension": once a job is automated, the worker receives a pension<sup><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup><sup><a href="#fn11" id="ref11">11</a></sup>. The pension must match the recipient's previous income and must not shrink to a subsistence stipend, repeating the "compensation" fallacy Marx identified under machinery<sup><a href="#fn12" id="ref12">12</a></sup>. Recipients should retain the right to continue creative work through publicly funded production. The goal is to decommodify artistic production so that the loss of commercial demand does not undermine creative capacity.

### Public and Cooperative Production Infrastructure

If major studios and platforms are expropriated, convert their production and distribution infrastructure into publicly owned utilities that guarantee access for independent and cooperative producers. Without that conversion, AI could eliminate commercial creative work while leaving creators without production or distribution resources, dependent on the entities that control the models.

## Can Productivity Be Seized Without Seizing the Biosphere?

### The Ecosocialist Indictment

The ecosocialist critique of AI rests on material facts, not ownership alone. NLP model training carries a substantial carbon footprint<sup><a href="#fn14" id="ref14">14</a></sup>. Training GPT-3 alone can directly evaporate roughly 700,000 liters of freshwater in U.S. data centers<sup><a href="#fn15" id="ref15">15</a></sup>. At one major hyperscaler, inference already accounts for roughly three-fifths of total machine-learning energy use against two-fifths for training<sup><a href="#fn16" id="ref16">16</a></sup>. Per-query energy costs also scale steeply with model size<sup><a href="#fn17" id="ref17">17</a></sup>, and the International Energy Agency projects that electricity demand from AI-focused data centers will roughly triple by 2030<sup><a href="#fn18" id="ref18">18</a></sup>. So running trained models to produce outputs (inference) at billions of queries per day may eventually exceed training in total energy use. Jevons's paradox applies: if AI becomes cheaper under socialism, total energy demand can rise even when each query uses less power<sup><a href="#fn19" id="ref19">19</a></sup>. The supply chain (rare earth mining, semiconductor fabrication, hardware e-waste) stays extractive whether data centers are public or private. Building a single AI system carries documented material labor and environmental costs, from mineral extraction through disposal<sup><a href="#fn20" id="ref20">20</a></sup>. Cobalt mining for the batteries and electronics in that chain has been linked to documented child labor and hazardous conditions in the Democratic Republic of the Congo<sup><a href="#fn21" id="ref21">21</a></sup>. Landlords and rentiers extract surplus from marginal sites in that chain regardless of who owns the data centers<sup><a href="#fn22" id="ref22">22</a></sup>.

A socialist program that expropriates data centers and expands AI infrastructure without imposing binding material limits constitutes social-democratic productivism rather than ecosocialism.

Ecosocialists could support socialist AI under the following conditions.

### Hard Energy Budgets

AI infrastructure should function within binding, democratically established carbon and water budgets. All training runs exceeding a specified energy threshold must undergo public environmental impact assessment and receive approval. Inference infrastructure should be subject to increasingly stringent efficiency standards. Energy for AI must be sourced from additional renewable capacity, rather than diverting existing clean energy from other sectors.

### Sufficiency Thresholds

Not everything that can be automated should be. A socialist AI program must distinguish socially necessary applications from socially wasteful ones. Necessary applications include medical diagnosis, materials science, ecosystem modeling, translation for cross-border worker communication, and accessibility services tools. Wasteful ones include AI-generated spam, personalized advertising, engagement-maximizing recommendation algorithms, and deepfake entertainment. Under capitalism, the profit motive tends to direct private AI investment toward the most immediately monetizable applications rather than necessarily toward the socially necessary ones listed above. AI infrastructure and consumer-facing products, for instance, have drawn the fastest-growing share of private AI funding in recent years<sup><a href="#fn23" id="ref23">23</a></sup>. No clean sector-by-sector breakdown of "wasteful" versus "necessary" spending as defined here exists, however, so this framing is this document's own extrapolation from that broader trend. Socialism must reverse that logic: democratic planning ought to prioritize the former and refuse to deploy the latter.

### Model Efficiency as a Design Requirement

The current trajectory toward larger models, increased computational demands, and higher energy consumption is well documented. Training compute for frontier models has doubled roughly every six months since the early 2010s<sup><a href="#fn24" id="ref24">24</a></sup>. Research has also found that model quality continues to improve with scale when model size and training data are increased together<sup><a href="#fn25" id="ref25">25</a></sup>. This document's own interpretation is that inter-firm competition, rather than technical necessity alone, drives firms to keep pushing that trajectory rather than pursuing efficiency-first alternatives. A publicly owned AI research infrastructure should mandate demonstrable efficiency improvements over previous models prior to deployment. Research into models that require less computation per query, such as low-energy architectures and sparse models, should be prioritized as a public good rather than treated as a cost center.

### Redirect AI Productivity Toward Ecological Restoration

Eskow contends that socialist AI should channel productivity gains toward both human and non-human flourishing, as well as reduced working hours<sup><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup>. The "productivity dividend" (the surplus generated by public ownership) should be allocated to ecological initiatives. These include climate simulation, biodiversity monitoring, materials science for clean energy, agricultural optimization to reduce chemical inputs, and industrial process redesign to minimize waste. Reduced working hours would enable human labor to be redirected toward ecological care work, such as reforestation, wetland restoration, and soil regeneration, which are labor-intensive.

### International Material Supply Chain Transformation

The semiconductor supply chain, from Congolese tantalum extraction to Taiwanese fabrication, cannot be rendered sustainable solely within national borders<sup><a href="#fn26" id="ref26">26</a></sup><sup><a href="#fn27" id="ref27">27</a></sup>. A socialist AI program should pursue international agreements on mining standards and worker protections and invest publicly in recycling and urban mining to reduce primary extraction. It should also support research into alternative materials that decrease reliance on conflict minerals and rare earth elements.

### Conditions for Non-Deployment

Ecosocialists would be correct to demand non-deployment of AI in the following cases:

- Autonomous weapons systems and military AI: ban them by international treaty, not simply "democratically controlled." Expropriation does not solve the problem if the public entity still supplies components for weapons systems.

- Mass surveillance infrastructure: predictive policing, border surveillance, social credit systems, and the workplace monitoring Eskow describes. Prohibit these outright; do not exclusively place them under "democratic control," because their existence creates capacities any future authoritarian turn could exploit.

- High-cost, low-value applications: when an application's energy costs surpass its social value, it should undergo case-by-case democratic assessment, with a default presumption against deployment absent clear social benefit.

- Engagement-manipulation systems: AI systems designed to manipulate human behavior for engagement, including recommendation algorithms, addictive design patterns, and attention-economy infrastructure. These systems constitute the core business model of platform companies such as Meta, TikTok/ByteDance, and ad-supported search and recommendation services, and should be dismantled rather than reformed.

## Minimum Program: A Consolidated Socialist AI Platform

### What to Expropriate

These assets should be transferred into democratic public ownership without compensation.

- Major AI corporations (OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Anthropic, Meta AI, Microsoft AI, etc.): full expropriation without compensation, transformed into publicly owned research and infrastructure utilities.

- Cloud infrastructure oligopolies (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud): transformed into public utilities under workers' control.

- Training data held by corporations: socialized, with sectoral agreement mechanisms for professional creative work.

- Chip fabrication capacity: brought under public ownership and international coordination, with production priorities shifted from military to civil applications.

### What to Resist

Prohibit these uses outright; democratic oversight alone is insufficient.

- Autonomous weapons and military AI: an international treaty, not simply national regulation.

- Mass surveillance systems: predictive policing, biometric tracking, workplace monitoring beyond occupational health and safety.

- Engagement-maximizing algorithms: addictive design, recommendation engines optimized for "time on platform."

- Non-consensual use of personal biometric and behavioral data for training.

- AI-generated material that impersonates specific individuals without consent (deepfake prohibitions with real enforcement).

### What to Build

These initiatives should be established as public goods under democratic public ownership.

- Public AI research infrastructure: fundamental research into efficient architectures, safety, and socially beneficial applications, with results in the public domain.

- Public content provenance and attribution infrastructure: training data registries, watermarking, and cryptographic signing as public goods.

- Publicly funded creative production: decommodified arts funding so that AI-driven productivity gains translate into more human creative capacity, not less.

- International scientific computing cooperatives: for climate simulation, epidemiological surveillance, materials science, and other global public goods.

- Democratically governed energy and resource budgets for all AI infrastructure, with binding caps and public audit.

### What to Guarantee Workers

Workers throughout the AI supply chain should be protected from displacement and exploitation.

- No layoffs due to AI: if productivity increases, hours decrease proportionally with no loss of pay<sup><a href="#fn12">12</a></sup>.

- Automation pensions at full previous income for workers in fully automated occupations, coupled with guaranteed retraining and redeployment options.

- Full workers' control over technology introduction: no AI deployment in any workplace without ratification by the affected workforce through rank-and-file committees.

- Sectoral collective bargaining rights over training data: professional creators' organizations control whether and how their members' work enters training data.

- Full labor rights for all AI supply chain workers, from Congolese cobalt miners to Kenyan content moderators<sup><a href="#fn28" id="ref28">28</a></sup> to Taiwanese fab workers: union recognition, health and safety, and international wage standards.

### What to Democratize

These decisions should be vested in democratic institutions rather than corporate boards or technocratic agencies.

- Algorithmic control: model deployment, training priorities, and allocation of funding.

- Public access to inference: AI capabilities as a universal service, not a subscription product.

- International coordination: the AI supply chain is irreducibly global, so governance must be international. That governance should emerge through the political mobilization of the international working class, not through agreements between capitalist states<sup><a href="#fn26">26</a></sup>.

## Open Questions for Organizers

### What to Expropriate First

The sequencing question matters politically. Three targets stand out as the most strategically logical first moves:

- Cloud infrastructure (AWS/Azure/GCP): the physical substrate on which everything else runs. As of mid-2026, these three providers alone account for roughly 63 percent of the global cloud infrastructure market<sup><a href="#fn29" id="ref29">29</a></sup>. That concentration gives control over compute an outsized influence over the direction of AI. Expropriating cloud infrastructure also has immediate benefits for public services (healthcare, education, government) and can win support from public-service unions.

- Chip fabrication: Without TSMC, Samsung, and Intel fabs, no AI models run. Leading-edge fabrication is especially concentrated: TSMC alone captured roughly 70 percent of global foundry revenue in 2025, with Samsung a distant second at around 7 percent.<sup><a href="#fn30" id="ref30">30</a></sup> This is the hardest target because production is geographically concentrated in Taiwan and South Korea, making it a question of international politics, not domestic expropriation alone.

- Training information and model weights (the learned parameters that define a model's behavior): legally complex but politically resonant. The "you stole our work" argument can unite artists, writers, and tech workers. A demand to socialize training data and open-source all publicly funded model weights could build a broad coalition.

### What to Resist First

The sequencing of prohibitions ought to give priority to those with the broadest coalition potential:

- Autonomous weapons: the clearest moral case, the broadest international support, and the highest catastrophic risk of inaction.

- Non-consensual biometric training data: directly affects every worker like the ones Eskow describes. This is likely to draw a broader coalition than sector-specific fights, since it maps onto a general public concern about unconsented data collection rather than a single industry. Enforceability, in turn, depends on the provenance infrastructure described above.

- AI-driven layoffs: a moratorium on job elimination through AI until democratic control structures are in place. This connects AI politics to the direct economic interests of the broadest section of the working class.

### What to Build in Public

Public alternatives should be developed immediately, even within the constraints of capitalism, both as demands and as prototypes.

- Open, auditable training data registries: build the infrastructure for transparency now, even under capitalism, as a demand and a prototype.

- Publicly owned core models (large general-purpose models created from large quantities of data): chatbots built for working-class people's political education rather than profit, in the spirit of collective agitational infrastructure<sup><a href="#fn31" id="ref31">31</a></sup>. Such projects would show that alternative ownership and purpose are technically feasible.

- Municipal and cooperative inference infrastructure: city-owned AI services for public needs (translation, accessibility, civic information) that show the public utility model.

### Unsettled Contradictions

- The one-country problem: Is "socialist AI in one country" possible when chips, rare earths, energy, and training data are globally distributed<sup><a href="#fn26">26</a></sup>? Almost certainly not, which means the AI question is inseparable from the question of world socialist revolution.

- The expertise problem: NNJ DSA Tech Committee members hold much of the technical knowledge this program depends on, yet democratic control requires that knowledge reach the general body. Even under democratic public ownership, the technical complexity of AI systems creates a structural power asymmetry between machine learning engineers and everyone else. How does democratic control function when most citizens cannot independently evaluate claims about model safety, bias, or capability? The problem is not unique to AI. It applies to nuclear energy, pharmaceutical regulation, and other technically complicated domains. AI is opaque and develops rapidly, which makes the asymmetry especially acute. Unlike simpler automated systems, the most capable models remain largely uninterpretable even to their own designers<sup><a href="#fn32" id="ref32">32</a></sup>, compounding the political challenge Lenin identified.<sup><a href="#fn33" id="ref33">33</a></sup>

- The international working class and the AI supply chain: the same global working class includes

   - cobalt miners in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC);

   - chip fabricators in Taiwan;

   - data labelers in Kenya<sup><a href="#fn34" id="ref34">34</a></sup>;

   - content moderators in the Philippines<sup><a href="#fn35" id="ref35">35</a></sup>; and

   - long-haul truck drivers in the US. Labor researchers project that automation will displace an estimated 294,000 jobs in trucking's highest-risk segment, long-distance highway routes, over the coming years. That same shift is expected to create other, lower-paid local delivery jobs in their place.<sup><a href="#fn36" id="ref36">36</a></sup>

- These workers are divided by nation, race, language, and markedly different conditions of exploitation. Achieving the political unity necessary for international democratic control of AI constitutes the central strategic challenge, one that cannot be resolved solely through improved policy documentation.

## Conclusion

While seizing AI's productivity under public ownership is necessary, the preceding analysis demonstrates that it is not sufficient. Expropriation without compensation eliminates corporate profiteering from collective and individual labor. On its own, though, it does not safeguard workers' biometric data, compensate authors whose work trains models, or ensure data centers operate within planetary boundaries. Comprehensive protections, including sectoral bargaining, automation pensions, energy budgets, and outright bans, must function collectively to address these challenges.

The Collective Chimera framework articulates a foundational principle: general social knowledge should be treated as a commons, while specific creative and personal contributions require consent and compensation. Consistent application of this principle precludes both extremes present under capitalism. It rejects the maximalist assertion that public exposure to a model permits unrestricted use, as well as the propertarian view that any personal data confers perpetual rights. A socialist AI program must maintain both halves of this distinction. Failure to do so risks either perpetuating corporate expropriation under new branding or instituting a data-ownership regime that replicates capitalist property relations within a nominally public system.

The unresolved issues identified here (the one-country problem, the expertise gap, and the fragmented composition of the international working class) are not mere deficiencies to be remedied. They constitute the central terrain of struggle. No policy platform can determine whether workers in Kinshasa, Taipei, Nairobi, Manila, and Newark will achieve the political unity necessary for international democratic control of AI. Such unity must be actively organized. This document provides the NNJ DSA Tech Committee and the broader chapter with a minimum program to pursue. The remaining task is to build the movement capable of realizing these objectives.
